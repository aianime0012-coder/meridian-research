AI spending is about to hit $2.59 trillion this year — up 47% from 2025
([Gartner, May 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-19-gartner-forecasts-worldwide-ai-spending-to-grow-47-percent-in-2026)).
More organizations than ever report using AI in at least one function. And yet:
according to BCG and MIT research, only about **26% of generative-AI
investments deliver measurable business value.**

That's not a technology problem. It's an operating problem. Here's what the
other 74% get wrong — and the fix that costs nothing but structure.

## The adoption paradox

Adoption keeps climbing. McKinsey's latest State of AI survey puts organizational
adoption at 72–78%, up from roughly a third in 2022. Gartner forecasts that 80%
of enterprises will have used generative-AI APIs or models by the end of 2026.

The spend is real. The ROI isn't keeping up.

The most telling data point is about **ownership**. In the Alice Labs
Implementation Index 2026 — a longitudinal dataset of 100+ enterprise AI builds —
**62% of stalled projects shared one root cause: no named business owner** with
a defined success metric and budget authority.

No owner. No metric. No deadline. That's the 74% in one line.

## Why pilots stall (the operating reasons)

Across stalled programs, the pattern is consistent:

1. **Nobody owns the outcome.** The CTO sponsors it, the vendor runs it, and
   the business unit treats it as IT's experiment. When "success" isn't
   defined by a business leader, no one is accountable for the number.
2. **Success is undefined.** "Improve efficiency" is not a metric. "Cut 5 hours
   per rep per week" is.
3. **The wrong problem gets automated.** Tools get bought for a use case that
   wasn't the actual bottleneck, because the diagnosis was skipped.
4. **Data quality is treated as someone else's problem.** Extraction fails at
   the mess, not the model.
5. **Change management is missing.** The best tool fails if the team won't adopt
   it — and adoption is a leadership job, not a tool feature.

None of these are solved by a bigger model. All of them are solved by process.

## The fix: three structural moves

### 1. Name an owner with a number

Before any pilot, assign one business owner with budget authority and a written
success metric. Examples:

- "Reduce per-rep document-processing time by 30% within 90 days."
- "Resolve 20% of tier-1 support tickets without a human within one quarter."
- "Cut vendor shortlisting effort from 3 weeks to 1 week, validated on 5 RFPs."

The metric is the contract. The Alice Labs data suggests the difference between
a stalled project and a shipped one is often literally this one decision.

### 2. Diagnose before you buy

Write the current-state process down. Count the steps. Measure the time.
The use case that looks most "AI-worthy" is rarely the highest-leverage one.
A quick readiness scan across data, skills, process, governance, and budget
surfaces where the real constraint is — and whether it's even an AI problem.

### 3. Time-box the pilot and pre-commit the decision

Run pilots in 4–6 weeks against the defined metric. Pre-commit: "if we hit the
bar, we scale; if we miss, we kill it with reasons logged." This is the
experiment discipline that separates operators from hobbyists — and it protects
you from the sunk-cost spiral.

## What this means for mid-market teams

For SMBs and mid-market organizations, the stakes are higher and the margin for
error thinner. You don't have a department to absorb a failed initiative. But
you also have the advantage: fewer stakeholders, faster decisions, and — if you
set it up right — a single accountable owner who can actually move.

The firms winning at AI aren't the ones buying the most tools. They're the ones
running the most disciplined experiments. Ownership, a metric, a deadline, a
kill-switch.
